This chapter presents the results produced by the \texttt{us\_inventory} and
\texttt{ads} archetypes described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, following the
material from the inventory that supplies the fuel cycle through the
transmutation it enables to the waste it leaves behind. It begins by
characterizing the U.S. spent fuel inventory as loaded by \texttt{us\_inventory},
confirming that the \gls{STANDARDS}~5.0.1 dataset is ingested correctly and
establishing the scale of material available. It then examines how the choice of
selection policy shapes the feed supplied to the burner, both in its delivery
over time and in its geographic provenance, before turning to the transmutation
performance of the \gls{ADS}: the actinide destroyed per cycle, the two burner
configurations considered, and the effect of recycling. Attention then moves to
the fate of the material once it has been processed, describing the composition
of the remaining inventory and the resulting waste stream. The chapter closes by
drawing these results together and interpreting what they imply for a
minor actinide transmutation mission.

\section{Inventory Characterization}
\label{sec:results-baseline}

Before any transmutation results are presented, this section characterizes the
inventory that the \texttt{us\_inventory} archetype ingests, establishing both
that the \gls{STANDARDS}~5.0.1 dataset is loaded correctly and the scale and
distribution of the material available to the fuel cycle. Because the inventory
load is deterministic, the same directory of assembly records always produces
the same pool. Therefore, these results depend only on the input data and the
parser, not on the simulation dynamics.

Reading the 75 assembly record files yields $315{,}976$ individual assemblies,
totalling $8.69\times10^{4}~\mathrm{t}$ of heavy metal, drawn from 61 facilities
for which geographic coordinates are available. Summing the minor actinide
content of every assembly, as defined in Equation~\ref{eq:usinv-ma-fraction},
gives a total accessible minor actinide inventory of $103.8~\mathrm{t}$. Minor
actinides account for only $0.12\%$ of the total heavy metal because discharged
light water reactor fuel contains relatively little neptunium, americium, and
curium. In terms of burner loading, the entire national minor actinide inventory
amounts to fewer than four $27~\mathrm{t}$ ADS~1 cores
(Table~\ref{tab:scenario-config}). This scarcity is the central constraint on
the transmutation mission and recurs throughout the results: the burner is
limited far more by the availability of minor actinides than by any reactor
parameter.

Figure~\ref{fig:results-source-map} shows the geographic distribution of this
material, with each facility placed at its coordinates and shaded by the mass of
spent fuel it supplies over the full simulation horizon. Because the run
draws down the accessible inventory, the drawn mass approaches the available inventory at each
site, so the figure doubles as a map of where the national inventory resides.
The distribution is strongly weighted toward the eastern and south-central
United States, where the largest single sites each supply on the order of
$2{,}500$--$2{,}700~\mathrm{t}$, while the smaller western sites contribute a few
hundred tonnes. This spatial concentration is the starting point for the
provenance analysis of Section~\ref{sec:results-policy}, since any policy that
draws assemblies in a particular order also implies a particular geographic
sequence of retrieval.

  \caption[Geographic distribution of the sourced U.S.\ spent fuel
  inventory.]{Geographic distribution of the spent nuclear fuel supplied by the

Establishing this baseline confirms that the archetype ingests the assembly-level
dataset faithfully and quantifies the material the transmutation fuel cycle has
to work with. The remainder of this chapter examines how that material is
selected (Section~\ref{sec:results-policy}), how it is transmuted in the
\gls{ADS} (Section~\ref{sec:results-ads}), and how the accessible inventory
evolves under recycling (Section~\ref{sec:results-evolution}).

\section{Effect of Selection Policy on the Feed}
\label{sec:results-policy}

The \texttt{us\_inventory} archetype selects individual assemblies by an explicit
ranking policy (Section~\ref{subsec:us-inventory-policies}), and that choice
determines the mass and composition of material delivered to separations and,
ultimately, to the burner. The selection mechanism acts on the static inventory
pool and is deterministic in the assembly ordering it produces: it does not
depend on downstream reactor dynamics. Its effect on the feed can therefore be
characterized directly, from the inventory data and from standalone
\texttt{us\_inventory} runs, independently of the full fuel cycle, and that is
how it is presented in this section. The production scenarios of
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} all use \texttt{highest\_minor\_actinide}
(Section~\ref{subsec:scenario-flowsheet}); the comparisons here show the benefit
of that choice relative to the alternatives.

\subsection{Delivery Trajectory}
\label{subsec:results-policy-trajectory}

Because every policy eventually draws from the same finite pool, the total mass
of minor actinides that any policy can deliver is identical: the cumulative curves all
terminate at the same $103.8~\mathrm{t}$
(Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}). What the policy changes is the
\emph{trajectory}, or how quickly minor actinides accumulate as assemblies are
drawn. Figure~\ref{fig:results-policy-trajectory} shows the cumulative minor
actinide delivered against the number of assemblies drawn, for the three
representative policies of set~A, with a horizontal line marking one
$27~\mathrm{t}$ ADS~1 core.

The separation is large. Ranking by \texttt{highest\_minor\_actinide} reaches a
full core after roughly $4.8\times10^{4}$ assembly draws, whereas
\texttt{lowest\_minor\_actinide} requires about $1.28\times10^{5}$, a factor of
$2.7$ more material handled for the same core. The unranked \texttt{first}
baseline falls between them, at about $7.7\times10^{4}$ draws, and its curve is
visibly segmented: because \texttt{first} draws facility by facility in load
order, its slope steps up and down as it moves through sites with different
minor actinide contents, whereas the ranked policies produce smooth,
monotonically curving trajectories. The timing here is expressed in assemblies
drawn rather than calendar time, since the mapping to time depends on the
retrieval throughput per time step, which is not the subject of this comparison.

This is the practical face of the scarcity established in
Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}. With the national inventory holding fewer
than four cores of minor actinide in total, the order of retrieval governs how
much spent fuel must be handled, and, as shown next, from how many sites, to
assemble even a single core.

\subsection{Geographic Provenance of the Feed}
\label{subsec:results-policy-provenance}

Because the selection order is also a geographic order, each policy implies a
different set of sites supplying the first core.
Figure~\ref{fig:results-policy-provenance} shows the minor actinide contributed
by each facility to the first $27~\mathrm{t}$ core under the set~A policies.
The contrast is stark. The \texttt{first} policy, drawing in load order,
concentrates the core in a handful of sites, each contributing on the order of
$2~\mathrm{t}$; \texttt{highest\_minor\_actinide} instead spreads the draw across
many facilities, drawing from assemblies with high minor actinide fractions
wherever they reside, so that no single site dominates. Ranking for feed quality
thus trades a geographically concentrated retrieval pattern for a distributed
one. This tradeoff connects directly to transportation and logistics, which fall outside the
scope of this work but are enabled by the provenance recorded in
\texttt{us\_inventorySupply} (Table~\ref{tab:usinv-supply}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/policy_provenance}
  \caption[Facility provenance of the first core by selection policy.]{Minor
  actinide contributed by each facility to the first $27~\mathrm{t}$ core, for
  the set~A policies. \texttt{first} concentrates the core in a few sites drawn
  in load order, while \texttt{highest\_minor\_actinide} distributes it across
  many facilities.}
  \label{fig:results-policy-provenance}
\end{figure}

The effect is not specific to ranking by minor actinide content. When the pool is
instead ordered by discharge age (\texttt{older}, \texttt{newer}) or by
reported burnup
(\texttt{highest\_burnup}, \texttt{lowest\_burnup}), the set of sites supplying
the first core shifts again, and each policy favors the facilities whose
assemblies rank highest under its criterion. This confirms that the archetype's
selection acts as a general lever on feed provenance: any physical property
recorded in the assembly data can be used to reshape both the composition and
the geographic origin of the material entering the fuel cycle.

A standalone \texttt{us\_inventory} run confirmed that the mechanism operates
identically within a full \Cyclus simulation and in an offline analysis of the
pool. The average composition of the supplied material followed the active
policy. Policies that ranked assemblies by plutonium content or burnup supplied
material with the correspondingly higher plutonium and fissile fractions.

\section{ADS Transmutation Performance}
\label{sec:results-ads}

This section examines how the \gls{ADS} transmutes the separated feed. Of the
eight runs defined in Section~\ref{subsec:scenario-runs}, most produce nearly
identical histories. As anticipated there, once-through and recycling operation
coincide whenever the burner is limited by its cycle duration rather than by feed
availability, so one representative run is sufficient for those cases. The
results therefore reduce to two comparisons. The first contrasts the core
containing only minor actinides (ADS~1) with the core containing equal masses of
plutonium and minor actinides (ADS~2) over the 100-year horizon, establishing how
core size and feed composition govern burner operation. The second compares
recycling with once-through operation for ADS~1, the only pairing in which the two
modes diverge. For a given burner configuration, the transmutation result for
each completed cycle is identical between modes and horizons; what changes is the
number of cycles completed before the burner exhausts its feed.

\subsection{Transmutation and Burn Fraction for Each Cycle}
\label{subsec:results-ads-burn}

At each discharge the core composition is read from the matched \textsc{Serpent}
case (Section~\ref{subsec:ads-discharge}), giving the surviving actinides and the
fission products produced during the cycle.
Figures~\ref{fig:results-case3-recycle-fpyield}
and~\ref{fig:results-case4-recycle-fpyield} show the cumulative fission product
yield of the two cores as a function of fission product mass number. Both display
the familiar double-humped distribution of low-to-moderate-energy actinide
fission, with a light peak near \(A \approx 100\) and a heavy peak near
\(A \approx 138\), separated by the symmetric fission valley. For ADS~1
(Figure~\ref{fig:results-case3-recycle-fpyield}), this signature appears even
though the feed contains no uranium or plutonium, confirming that the minor
actinides themselves undergo fission in the fast, subcritical core. The
double-humped distribution arises from the shell structure of the fission
fragments rather than from one particular actinide species.
In both cases the three burnup snapshots nearly overlie one another, since the
cumulative yield shape is set by the actinide mixture, which shifts only slowly
with irradiation.

The two cores differ in the details of the distribution, and the difference
reflects their feed compositions. ADS~2 contains a substantial plutonium
fraction. Fission of \({}^{239}\mathrm{Pu}\) proceeds through a heavier compound
nucleus than fission of the actinides that dominate the ADS~1 feed. This shifts
the light peak slightly upward in mass and partially fills the symmetric fission
valley, reducing the peak-to-valley ratio. ADS~1, which contains a lighter mixture
dominated by americium and neptunium, shows a marginally deeper valley and a light
peak shifted slightly lower. The difference is subtle because both feeds fission
in the same fast spectrum and span overlapping actinide masses, but it is the
expected distinction between feeds with and without plutonium. It also confirms
that the reduced-order model carries the difference in composition through to the
fission product inventory.

The mass accounting for each cycle is summarized by the burn fraction, defined
as the ratio of actinide mass destroyed to actinide mass charged. For ADS~1, this
fraction remains constant at \(41.3\%\) across all cycles
(Figure~\ref{fig:results-case3-recycle-percycle}). This behavior follows directly
from the reduced-order model: each core is irradiated to the same point on the
same depletion trajectory, so every cycle removes the same fraction of its
charge. For ADS~2, the burn fraction rises to just under \(50\%\)
(Figure~\ref{fig:results-case4-recycle-percycle}) because the plutonium in the
feed fissions more readily than the actinides that dominate the ADS~1 feed.

The burn fraction reported here is the net reduction in total actinide mass,
calculated from the \texttt{ActinideDestroyed} event according to
Equation~\ref{eq:ads-destroyed}. Equation~\ref{eq:ads-ratio} first determines the
mass retained in the discharged product, after which the archetype subtracts the
total actinide mass remaining in that product from the total actinide mass in the
matched case's initial loading. Because plutonium is included in the total
actinide inventory, minor actinides converted into plutonium remain in
\(M_{\mathrm{out}}a_c(t_{\mathrm{irr}})\) and are not counted as destroyed. The
reported value therefore represents net actinide destruction in the tracked
depletion inventory, rather than the disappearance of minor actinides alone.

This destruction rate can be checked against first principles. Fissioning one
kilogram of actinide releases approximately \(0.93~\mathrm{GW\text{-}d}\) of
thermal energy, so a \(3000~\mathrm{MW_{th}}\) core destroys about
\(3.2~\mathrm{kg}\) of actinide per full-power day, or roughly
\(1{,}120~\mathrm{kg\,yr^{-1}}\) at full power. Applied to the ten-year
ADS~1 cycle, this predicts destruction close to the
\(11{,}150~\mathrm{kg}\) read from the \textsc{Serpent} case, agreeing to within
a few percent. The reduced-order transmutation model is therefore consistent with
the fission energy balance, an independent validation of the archetype's mass
accounting.

\subsection{Core Size and Feed Composition: the Two Burner Cases}
\label{subsec:results-ads-cases}

The two burner configurations produce very different operating histories, driven
entirely by core size and the resulting cycle length.
Figures~\ref{fig:results-case3-recycle-dutycycle}
and~\ref{fig:results-case4-recycle-dutycycle} show the core state, irradiating or
idle, over the 100-year horizon for the two cases.

ADS~1 has a large core loading of \(27~\mathrm{t}\) and is irradiated for
ten years per cycle. Over 100 years of recycling it completes only five cycles and
then falls idle for the remainder of the horizon, reaching a duty factor of
\(50\%\). The core stops not because the horizon ends, but because the quantity
of recoverable neptunium, americium, and curium accumulated in its feed buffer
can no longer reach the required \(27~\mathrm{t}\) core loading.
This does not mean that no actinide remains in the fuel cycle: some surviving
actinide has been converted to plutonium, which is not returned to the
ADS~1 feed, while additional material remains in discharged
products, waste, and facility buffers. ADS~2, by contrast, has a small core
loading of \(4.4~\mathrm{t}\) and is cycled every two years. It completes 42
cycles and runs nearly continuously across the full horizon at a duty factor of
\(85\%\).
It never exhausts its feed because it consumes minor actinides slowly and
supplements them with the far more abundant plutonium.

This is the central distinction between the two configurations. ADS~1 is
limited by feed availability: its throughput depends on how much minor actinide
the inventory can supply rather than on the physical capability of the reactor.
ADS~2 is instead limited by its cycle duration. It processes material as quickly
as its operating cycle allows, and the inclusion of plutonium provides enough
feed to sustain that operation for a century. The tradeoff is that ADS~2 is no
longer dedicated solely to minor actinide destruction. Approximately half of the
material it consumes is plutonium, so it addresses the broader transuranic
inventory. Over the full simulation horizon, ADS~2 destroys
\(91.8~\mathrm{t}\) of actinides, compared with \(55.8~\mathrm{t}\) for
ADS~1, because it burns plutonium together with the minor actinides.

For ADS~2, the once-through and recycling runs are indistinguishable because
operation is limited by the two-year cycle duration. Little surviving actinide
is recovered before the next core loading is due, so recycling changes almost
nothing. This result is why only one ADS~2 run is shown.

\subsection{Recycling versus Once-Through Operation for ADS~1}
\label{subsec:results-ads-recycle}

ADS~1 is the only configuration in which recycling materially changes the
outcome because its operation is limited by feed availability. Comparing the
recycling history by cycle (Figure~\ref{fig:results-case3-recycle-percycle}) with
the once-through history (Figure~\ref{fig:results-case3-oncethrough-percycle})
isolates this effect. The two runs produce the same result for each completed
cycle: a \(27~\mathrm{t}\) charge, \(11.15~\mathrm{t}\) of net actinide
destruction, and a \(41.3\%\) burn fraction. The depletion transformation does
not depend on the source of the feed. What differs is the number of cycles the
burner completes before the feed is exhausted: three under once-through
operation and five under recycling.

The cumulative panels make the consequence concrete. In once-through mode, the
burner charges \(81~\mathrm{t}\) in total and records
\(33.5~\mathrm{t}\) of net actinide destruction. With recycling, it charges
\(135~\mathrm{t}\) and records \(55.8~\mathrm{t}\), an increase of
\(1.67\times\) in actinide destroyed. Recycling achieves this by
recovering the surviving minor actinides from each discharge and returning them to
the feed, so the same atoms make more than one pass through the core and the
burner sustains operation past the point where the once-through supply is spent.

The total charged mass provides the clearest evidence of reuse. The accessible
minor actinide inventory is only \(103.8~\mathrm{t}\)
(Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}), yet the recycling run charges
\(135~\mathrm{t}\), more minor actinide material than is present in the national
inventory. This is possible only because recycling returns material for
additional passes; the excess of roughly \(31~\mathrm{t}\) represents material
irradiated more than once. The once-through run, by contrast, charges \(81~\mathrm{t}\), comfortably
within the inventory, and simply stops when the accessible feed is gone.

Even with recycling, however, the burner still stops well short of the horizon.
The initial \(103.8~\mathrm{t}\) minor actinide inventory is only about
\(3.8\) fresh core loadings. After each irradiation, only the surviving
neptunium, americium, and curium is eligible to return to the ADS~1 feed; actinide
converted to plutonium is routed outside that feed stream, and the archetype will
not load a partial core. Recycling therefore extends the campaign from three
cores to five, but eventually the amount of recoverable minor actinide material
remains below the \(27~\mathrm{t}\) loading threshold.

\subsection{Transmutation Rate and Mission Scale}
\label{subsec:results-ads-rate}

Taken together, these results frame the scale of the transmutation mission. A
single \(3000~\mathrm{MW_{th}}\) unit destroys actinides at up to about
\(1{,}120~\mathrm{kg\,yr^{-1}}\) at full power, with the realized rate reduced
in proportion to the duty factor. Over a 30-year mission, ADS~2, which runs
nearly continuously, destroys on the order of \(27~\mathrm{t}\) of actinides per
unit. ADS~1 destroys less because it stalls when its feed is exhausted. The two
simulation horizons reinforce this distinction: a core limited by its cycle
duration produces roughly in proportion to the mission length, whereas a core
limited by feed availability plateaus after the accessible inventory is consumed.
This is why extending the ADS~1 simulation from 30 to 100 years adds only two
cycles.

The consequence is that sizing a fleet to destroy the minor actinide inventory is
not the usual problem of determining reactor capacity. The entire national minor
actinide inventory represents fewer than four core loadings. The limitation is
how quickly that finite inventory can be retrieved, separated, and recycled, not
how much reactor power is installed. Additional units could shorten the calendar
time required to process the available feed, but they could not destroy more
minor actinide material than the inventory contains. A configuration that also
burns plutonium, as ADS~2 does, avoids this feed limitation but redefines the
mission from minor actinide destruction to bulk transuranic destruction. This
shift from a mission limited by installed power to one limited by feed
availability is the central practical finding of the transmutation results and is
examined further in Section~\ref{sec:results-discussion}.

\section{Processed Inventory}
\label{sec:results-evolution}

The transmutation results of Section~\ref{sec:results-ads} describe what the
burner does in each cycle; this section follows the material through the whole fuel
cycle and asks what becomes of the national inventory once it is processed. Every
kilogram drawn from \texttt{us\_inventory} meets one of three fates: it is
destroyed by fission in the \gls{ADS}, it is recovered and returned to the feed
under recycling, or it is routed to disposal. Tracking these streams shows both
what the mission accomplishes and what it leaves behind, and connects the results
back to the waste management motivation of Chapter~\ref{ch:background}.

\subsection{Disposition of the Minor Actinide Inventory}
\label{subsec:results-fate-ma}

Table~\ref{tab:results-ma-fate} closes the actinide mass balance against the
\(103.8~\mathrm{t}\) of minor actinides initially present in the accessible
inventory (Section~\ref{sec:results-baseline}). The quantity labeled
``undestroyed descendant actinide'' is the initial actinide mass minus the net
actinide destruction recorded by the ADS. It is therefore not equivalent to
feed of minor actinides still available to the burner: it includes any surviving
neptunium, americium, and curium, actinides produced by transmutation such as
plutonium, material routed to waste, and material held in facility buffers.

In once-through operation the burner records \(33.5~\mathrm{t}\) of net actinide
destruction and leaves \(70.3~\mathrm{t}\) of descendant actinide undestroyed. Of
that remainder, roughly \(47.5~\mathrm{t}\) is surviving actinide discharged to
waste, while about \(22.8~\mathrm{t}\) remains in unprocessed inventory because
it is insufficient to form another \(27~\mathrm{t}\) core.

With recycling, net actinide destruction increases to \(55.8~\mathrm{t}\), leaving
\(48.0~\mathrm{t}\) of descendant actinide somewhere in the modeled fuel cycle.
The \(48.0~\mathrm{t}\) value does not imply that another \(27~\mathrm{t}\)
ADS~1 core could be loaded. In the ADS~1 flowsheet, only Np, Am, and Cm
are recovered as burner feed; plutonium produced during irradiation is routed
outside the minor actinide feed stream, and the remaining recoverable
minor actinide inventory is distributed among products and buffers. The ADS
therefore stalls when its feed buffer cannot again accumulate a full
\(27~\mathrm{t}\) loading.

\subsection{Representative ADS Discharge Compositions}
\label{subsec:results-fate-waste}

Figure~\ref{fig:results-ads1-discharge} shows the isotopic composition of one
representative \(27~\mathrm{t}\) discharge from ADS~1. The composition is
extracted from the ADS output received by the downstream separations facility
before that material is partitioned or combined with another stream. It
therefore excludes the uranium, plutonium, and fission products removed from the
original spent fuel during front-end separation. This distinction is important:
the previous repository inventory analysis combined the ADS discharge with the
much larger front-end separations reject stream and did not represent the
material leaving the ADS alone.

The discharge contains surviving neptunium, americium, and curium, together with
fission products and actinides generated during irradiation. Although the
initial ADS~1 loading contains only neptunium, americium, and curium, plutonium
isotopes, particularly \({}^{238}\mathrm{Pu}\), appear prominently in the
discharge. This plutonium is not material carried into the core from the
front-end reject stream. The discharged composition is obtained from the full
\textsc{Serpent} depletion inventory, in which neutron-induced transmutation
and radioactive decay chains convert part of the initial minor actinide
inventory into descendant plutonium isotopes and small quantities of uranium.
These products remain part of the discharged actinide inventory and are
therefore not counted as destroyed actinide.

Under recycling, the downstream separations facility recovers plutonium,

from the recycle stream and routed to waste. ADS~2 therefore retains plutonium
within the burner cycle rather than routing it directly outside the burner feed,
which allows it to address the broader transuranic inventory rather than minor
actinides alone.

For ADS~1, the total actinide inventory decreases from
\(27.00~\mathrm{t}\) in the initial core to approximately
\(15.84~\mathrm{t}\) in the discharge. The remaining mass appears primarily as
fission products and other non-actinides. The redistribution among the actinide
groups is also significant: neptunium, americium, and curium decrease, while
plutonium is produced. The decrease of an individual minor actinide group is
therefore larger than the net actinide destruction because part of that material
is converted into another actinide rather than fissioned.

ADS~2 reduces the actinide mass from \(4.418~\mathrm{t}\) to approximately
\(2.231~\mathrm{t}\) per cycle, corresponding to a \(49.5\%\) net reduction.
Its larger fractional reduction reflects the plutonium-bearing feed, but the
discharge still contains substantial plutonium and minor actinides that require
recovery and additional irradiation for further destruction. These comparisons
show that neither burner eliminates its actinide loading in one pass. Instead,
each converts approximately \(41\)--\(50\%\) of the loaded actinide mass per
cycle, making the availability and performance of the downstream separations
and recycle system central to the overall mission.

Taken together, these results qualify what transmutation achieves for the national
inventory. Over a century, even the more favorable recycling configuration
destroys only about half of the accessible minor actinides. Achieving that result
requires processing essentially the entire spent fuel inventory. Most of the
processed material, including uranium, fission products, and, for ADS~1,
plutonium, still requires disposal. The mission changes the isotopic character of
the waste by removing minor actinides, which contribute strongly to its long-term
radiotoxicity and heat load, but it does not substantially reduce the bulk mass
requiring management and disposal. Quantifying the resulting reduction in
radiotoxicity or decay heat would require coupling these mass histories to a decay
and dose calculation. That analysis is beyond the scope of this work but is a
natural extension discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}. The fuel cycle
results instead establish the upstream reality against which any such benefit
must be weighed: minor actinide transmutation is constrained not by reactor
performance but by the scarcity of its feed and the scale of the inventory that
must be handled to recover it.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:results-discussion}

The results of this chapter can be read as a single argument about scale. The
\texttt{us\_inventory} and \texttt{ads} archetypes were built to connect a
detailed, assembly-resolved picture of the U.S. spent fuel inventory to a
reduced-order model of an accelerator-driven burner, and running them together
turns qualitative expectations about minor actinide transmutation into quantified
constraints. Two findings dominate, and both point to the same conclusion: the
scale and dilution of the national inventory, not the capability of the burner,
set the limits of what transmutation can achieve. The first is that the
mission is limited by the availability of its minor actinide feed rather than by
installed reactor capacity. The second is that, because that feed is such a
small fraction of the spent fuel, the mission changes the composition of the
waste far more than its quantity. In each case the binding factor lies in the
fuel cycle required to retrieve, separate, and handle a large, dilute inventory
to reach a scarce target material, not in the accelerator-driven system that
consumes it.

\subsection{The Mission is Feed-Bound}
\label{subsec:discussion-feedbound}

The central result is that a minor actinide transmutation mission is limited by
the availability of its feed rather than by installed reactor capacity. The
accessible minor actinide inventory is \(103.8~\mathrm{t}\), fewer than four
loadings of a single \(27~\mathrm{t}\) core. The binding question is therefore
not how much power can be deployed, but how quickly this finite, dilute inventory
can be retrieved, separated, and recycled. ADS~1 demonstrates this limitation: it
stalls after three cores under once-through operation or five with recycling
(Section~\ref{subsec:results-ads-recycle}). Increasing reactor power cannot
change that outcome because sufficient feed for additional cores does not exist.
This inverts the usual framing of reactor deployment, in which capacity is the
constraint and fuel is assumed abundant.

A simple rate calculation frames the fleet question that follows from this. A
\(3000~\mathrm{MW_{th}}\) unit destroys actinide at up to about
\(1{,}120~\mathrm{kg\,yr^{-1}}\) at full power
(Section~\ref{subsec:results-ads-rate}), so clearing the \(103.8~\mathrm{t}\)
inventory represents on the order of \(90\) unit-years of operation at full
power. Distributed over a 30-year mission, this is roughly three units' worth of
destruction capacity. A single unit would require most of a century, and ADS~1
could not operate at full duty for that long because of limited feed availability.
This is not a precise fleet sizing estimate because it omits reprocessing losses,
retrieval and transportation logistics, and the reduction in duty factor imposed
by feed availability. It nevertheless establishes the order of magnitude: the
transmutation of the national minor actinide inventory would require a handful of
high-power units operating over several decades, and its pace would be set by fuel
handling rather than by the number of units constructed.

ADS~2 avoids the feed limitation, but only by changing the mission. Plutonium
is approximately ten times more abundant in the inventory than minor actinides,
and burning it together with minor actinides provides enough feed for the small
core to operate for the full century at an \(85\%\) duty factor. ADS~2 destroys
\(91.8~\mathrm{t}\) of actinides, compared with \(55.8~\mathrm{t}\) for ADS~1
(Section~\ref{subsec:results-ads-cases}). However, roughly half of the ADS~2 feed
is plutonium, so it functions as a transuranic burner rather than a dedicated
minor actinide burner. The choice between the configurations is therefore a
choice of mission: destroy scarce minor actinides with high radiotoxicity at a
rate bounded by their availability, or destroy the broader transuranic inventory
at a higher rate by accepting plutonium as feed.

\subsection{Transmutation Changes Waste Composition}
\label{subsec:discussion-character}

The second finding concerns what the mission does to the waste. The
representative input--output comparisons in
Figures~\ref{fig:results-ads1-before-after}
and~\ref{fig:results-ads2-before-after} show that neither burner eliminates
its actinide loading in one irradiation pass. ADS~1 reduces the loaded
actinide mass by \(41.3\%\) per cycle, while ADS~2 reduces it by
\(49.5\%\). The remaining actinides require further separation and recycle
if additional destruction is desired.

These per-cycle reductions describe the performance of the burner itself,
but they do not translate into a comparable reduction in the total mass
requiring disposal. Minor actinides constitute only \(0.12\%\) of the spent
fuel, so recovering the approximately \(100~\mathrm{t}\) accessible inventory
requires separations to process on the order of the entire drawn spent fuel
inventory. Uranium and fission products removed during front-end separation
therefore dominate the disposal stream. In the ADS~1 fuel cycle, plutonium
produced from the minor actinide feed is also routed outside the ADS~1 recycle
stream. The principal modeled effect of transmutation is consequently a change
in isotopic composition through the destruction and redistribution of
actinides, rather than a substantial reduction in the bulk mass routed to
waste (Section~\ref{sec:results-evolution}).

Repository volume, requirements for waste packages, and effects on the disposal
footprint were not evaluated in this work. The modeled transmutation process
removes minor actinides, which contribute strongly to the long-term radiotoxicity
and decay heat of spent fuel. This is a meaningful outcome for repository
performance, but it changes the composition of the waste rather than substantially
reducing its mass, and it requires handling the full inventory.

This reframes the value proposition of transmutation in a way the fuel cycle
model is well suited to expose. The benefit is not a reduction in waste mass, but the removal of long-lived
constituents. The cost is a fuel cycle that must retrieve,
transport, and reprocess essentially the entire national inventory to recover a
trace component. Whether that trade is worthwhile is a question about repository
requirements and reprocessing capacity, not about the burner itself.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:discussion-limitations}

Several limitations constrain the interpretation of these results, and identifying
them also marks the most useful directions for further work.

The \gls{ADS} model is reduced-order. The burner does not solve neutron transport
during the simulation; it reads precomputed \textsc{Serpent} depletion cases,
selects the nearest case based on power and input composition, and interpolates
along the selected case's irradiation time axis
(Section~\ref{sec:ads-archetype}). This approach is appropriate for a fuel cycle
study, where the quantity of interest is the mass transmuted per core rather than
the detailed physics within the core. The destruction rate for each cycle was
shown to agree with an independent fission energy balance to within a few percent
(Section~\ref{subsec:results-ads-burn}). The model nevertheless inherits the
assumptions of the underlying cases, including a single homogeneous fuel region,
a fixed geometry, and a library spanning only the two representative feeds used
here. It cannot capture behavior outside the tabulated cases. A core whose
composition drifts far from any library case is matched to the nearest one rather
than modeled directly. The constant burn fraction from cycle to cycle results
from irradiating every core to the same point on the same trajectory, not a physical invariant.

The inventory selection results characterize the feed directly from the static
inventory rather than from the coupled simulation
(Section~\ref{sec:results-policy}). This treatment is exact because the selection
is a deterministic ordering of a fixed pool. However, the policy comparison
isolates the supply side and does not reflect any feedback between selection and burner
operation. All production scenarios use the single \texttt{highest\_minor\_actinide}
policy, so the effect of policy on outcomes for the full fuel cycle, rather than
only on the feed, is left for future work.

A more fundamental system level limitation is that the enabling separations
infrastructure is assumed rather than evaluated. Both burner configurations
require the national spent fuel inventory to be reprocessed before the ADS can
receive its feed, and ADS~2 additionally requires plutonium and minor actinides
to be recovered as separate streams and blended in a prescribed ratio. The
technical readiness, recovery losses, product quality, safeguards requirements,
cost, and policy acceptability of such an infrastructure may be at least as
consequential to deployment as the performance of the burner itself. The
present simulations therefore should not be interpreted as demonstrating the
feasibility of the complete transmutation system; they evaluate its material
flows after assuming that the necessary separations capability exists.

The study also does not include a reprocessing only reference case. It therefore
cannot isolate how much of a potential repository benefit would arise from
partitioning uranium, plutonium, minor actinides, and fission products into
separate streams, and how much additional benefit would result specifically
from irradiation in the ADS. That distinction would require applying the same
decay heat, radiotoxicity, and disposal analysis to an unreprocessed case, a
reprocessing only case, and the reprocessing plus transmutation cases.

Finally, the study stops at the mass balance. It quantifies what is destroyed and
what remains but does not evaluate the radiological or economic consequences that
determine whether the mission is worthwhile. Radioactive decay is modeled within
the simulation, so material compositions are aged as they move through the fuel
cycle. However, no downstream radiotoxicity or decay heat calculation is applied
to the final waste compositions, so the reduction in long-term radiological
hazard that motivates minor actinide removal is not quantified here. Likewise, no cost,
facility, or transportation models accompany the fuel cycle, so the substantial
reprocessing and handling burden implied by the results is described but not
priced. The model also does not represent the
retrieval and transportation logistics for which the provenance results
(Section~\ref{subsec:results-policy-provenance}) provide useful inputs.

These omissions identify important directions for future work. The
assembly-resolved provenance records, mass-conserving discharge streams, and
operating histories developed here provide the inputs required for subsequent
radiotoxicity, economic, transportation, and siting analyses. These extensions
are discussed further in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}.
